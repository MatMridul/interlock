# PSYCHOLOGY: why the screen behaves the way it does

Scope: this maps well-documented perception and interaction principles to concrete decisions for one product, a screen that shows an AI agent proposing actions while a small state machine decides what runs. Evidence strength differs by principle. Where I say *qualitative*, the principle is a design heuristic with mixed or modest experimental support, and I do not claim an effect size. Tests below are checks on screenshots or a five-minute usability pass with 3 to 5 people, not experiments.

**The one thing the viewer must understand in 10 seconds:** a proposal is not an action; the machine's verdict is. Everything below serves that.

| Principle (what it says, evidence) | Decision it drives | How we test it |
|---|---|---|
| **Response-time bands** (Miller 1968; Nielsen's 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s summary of them). About 0.1 s reads as direct cause and effect; about 1 s keeps the flow of thought; about 10 s loses attention. Doherty's 400 ms is a productivity rule of thumb: *qualitative*. | Every user action gets visible feedback within 100 ms (button press, token spawn starts). The whole proposal-to-verdict beat is 2.0 to 2.3 s, so it is a visible event, not a delay. A turn that takes longer shows "agent is working" (an honest wait state), not a spinner pretending to progress. | Frame sampling: first visual change after click is under 100 ms. Count beat length in the strip images. |
| **Preattentive attributes** (Treisman; Wolfe). Motion, colour contrast, size and orientation are detected before conscious search. Robust for single features. | Only two things ever get saturated colour and motion: the travelling proposal and a blocked event. Red appears nowhere else. The amber of the human control appears nowhere else. | Squint test on every screenshot: do the two pops identify themselves? Count saturated hues per frame (target: at most 3). |
| **Common fate and Gestalt grouping** (Wertheimer; proximity, continuity, closure). Elements that move together are seen as one thing. | The token, its trail and the arc along the conduit move as one object; the afterglow stays on the conduit that was used. Scoreboard cells sit together; the human control is spatially apart from the agent console (proximity says "different actor"). Conduits are continuous curves, so the eye follows source to target. | Freeze-one-frame test (living-ui): does one still frame tell proposal from verdict? Frame strips judged by someone who has not seen the build. |
| **Working-memory limits** (Miller 7±2; Cowan about 4 chunks). The 7±2 figure is overstated; about 4 is the better estimate. | The machine has 4 live states and 6 symbols, so all of it fits on screen at once with no scrolling and no hidden states. The sink is one object, not an extra state. The scoreboard has four numbers. Attack vectors are six short names, not a menu tree. | Count simultaneously visible distinct objects. Ask a tester to name the 4 states after 10 seconds. |
| **von Restorff isolation effect** (1933). The odd item out is remembered. | Two isolated items: the human-approval control (own colour, own frame, the only physical-metaphor object) and a blocked event (the only thing that is struck through, stamped or hatched). | Ask: what was different on that screen? Expected answers: the human key, the refusals. |
| **Fitts's law** (Fitts 1954; robust). Time to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with size. | The six attack vectors are full-width rows (large targets, one column, adjacent to the transcript); keys 1 to 6 fire them. The human control is deliberately a smaller, deliberate target (a lifted cover or a dialog) because a slip must be hard. | Measure travel from composer to any vector: one column, under 2 target-heights apart. |
| **Hick's law** (Hick; Hyman 1952). Choice time grows with the log of the number of equal options. | Six vectors, grouped by name, plus one primary action (Autoplay / Perform / Run). Legit moves are three chips, not another list. No settings screen on the stage. | Five-second task: "make the agent try to skip approval". Count hesitations. |
| **Zeigarnik effect and goal-gradient** (Zeigarnik 1927 replicates inconsistently; goal-gradient: Hull 1932, Kivetz 2006). Unfinished tasks stay in mind; effort rises near a goal. Both *qualitative* for UI. | The transfer workflow is a visible line: START, AUTHED, DRAFTED, APPROVED, with the next legal move lit in the ledger. The viewer sees what is still missing (a human approval) without reading. | Ask what has to happen next after the draft. |
| **Norman: signifiers, feedback, mapping, constraints** (Norman 1988/2013). | Signifiers: a cover on the key switch, a hazard frame, a drawn hatch for blocked. Mapping: position on screen = position in the workflow (left to right). Feedback: every control answers in under 100 ms. Constraint: the agent console has no control that looks like it can approve; the human control is structurally outside it. | Ask a tester which control can approve; the answer must not be "the chat". |
| **Aesthetic-usability effect** (Kurosu and Kashimura 1995; Tractinsky 2000). Attractive interfaces are judged easier to use, partly regardless of actual ease. | Distinctive, coherent craft in each comp helps the first impression, but it is never a substitute for legibility: every pair is contrast-checked (see ROUND_1.md). Beauty buys attention; the honesty rules keep trust. | Contrast script; usability pass must succeed with the intro and decoration off. |
| **Peak-end rule** (Kahneman et al. 1993; consistent for recalled experiences). People remember the peak and the end. | Design both on purpose. Peak: the blocked proposal (shudder, shatter, drain into the sink) and the human key turning. End: a closing state that shows the evidence, not a flourish: scoreboard with *Side effects from blocked: 0*, tape with refused cells above it, "executed trace is in L(M)". The autoplay ends on that screen and holds. | Watch the last 5 seconds of the autoplay: is the final frame the proof? |
| **Cross-modal congruence** (Spence 2011; Marks). Sound, touch and sight that agree in timing and meaning are read as one event. | One grammar, one timing family (MOTION_GRAMMAR.md): allowed is one short, high, soft event; blocked is two short low ones; human approval is the one long one. Sound and haptics are off by default, user-toggled, and mirror the visual, never carry meaning alone. | Mute test: everything is still understood with sound and haptics off. |
| **Attention economy and change blindness** (Rensink 1997; Simons). Simultaneous changes go unseen; ambient motion competes with the signal. | One primary animation at a time (queue, never overlap). Ambient motion (breathing lamps, grain) is slow, low-contrast and pauses when the tab is hidden or reduced motion is on. Backlogs speed up, never drop. | Count moving things in any frame: one primary plus decoration only. |
| **Calibrated trust** (Lee and See 2004, trust in automation; qualitative). Trust should match capability: neither over- nor under-trust. | Never claim more than is true: no "zero hallucination", no "mathematically proven safety". Show measured latency only when the server sent it; show the limits (what is not guaranteed) one tap away; label canned data as canned. Show the *executed log* as the source of truth, not the model's words. | Copy review against SPEC_GATE_v2.md section 1; every number in the UI has a source. |

## Consequences that every comp must keep
1. The proposal is visibly *untrusted* (neutral, dashed or cartouche) until the verdict colours it.
2. A blocked move never changes the state, and the screen shows that by not moving the lit state.
3. The human control is a different kind of object from everything the agent can touch.
4. Text, shape and position carry every meaning; colour only reinforces.
5. No effect sizes are claimed here. Where a principle is contested, the decision still stands on usability grounds, and the test above is how we find out whether it worked.
